* Introduction
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: introduction
:END:
Perseus has been encoding Greek and Latin texts, critical commentary,
and lexica since the 1980s, first in SGML and then in XML, using
schemas based on the Guidelines of the Text Encoding Initiative.
Perseus's text encoding has been and continues to be ground-breaking
and innovative, as Perseus's researchers investigate the affordances
of machine-readable text, and the variety of its methods can be found
throughout the corpora today.

As Perseus develops a new generation of research tools and reading
environments, it is working through its texts to normalize their
encoding and produce a controlled schema and encoding guidelines. This
standardization will greatly simplify automated large-scale
processing, which has often been hampered by the number and variety of
edge cases.  This project will result in a new Perseus edition, whose
encoded texts will be distinct from earlier versions (which will
continue to be available indefinitely as legacy editions). (See [[https://github.com/PerseusDLCode/MinimumViablePerseus/discussions/56][discussion]].)

This normalization takes three forms:

- Structural Normalization :: Perseus has exercised many strategies to
  encode the structure of written works.  Some encodings have
  attempted simply to capture the /logical structure/ of a work,
  independent of printed source text: its generic sequences of prose
  blocks and verse lines, organized into equally generic divisions
  (chapters; scenes; stanzas).  Others have tried to encode both the
  /logical structure/ of the work and the /physical structure/ of the
  source edition: line breaks; pagination; headings; notes and
  annotations.

  Several years ago, Perseus experimented with adopting the EpiDoc
  schema for all of its texts, regardless of source or genre, in an
  attempt to standardize its encoded structures with a ready-made
  TEI customization.  EpiDoc was developed to encode inscriptions and
  papyri, however, and has turned out to be a less-than-perfect fit
  for printed books.  Perseus is now developing its own TEI
  customizations (the approved way of using the TEI's collections of
  elements and attributes for serious encoding projects) based on the
  logical structure of various text genres.  These customizations form
  a family of schemas that will be applied to encoded texts based on
  their genre.

  Because so many of the works in Perseus's corpora are well known,
  and because many of them already encode generic structural patterns,
  we have been able to develop tools (found in this repository) to
  assign schemas to about 90% of the corpus automatically.  The
  remaining texts are few in number, and we anticipate human editors
  will have no trouble assigning appropriate schemas to them.

- Semantic Normalization :: The TEI is notorious for providing an
  abundance of elements and attributes encoders may use to capture
  textual semantics.  Perseus's encoders have experimented with them
  all, in pursuit of encodings that enable them to process texts for
  particular purposes.  For the Perseus Project, those purposes have
  been primarily hypertextual and linguistic: to instantiate links
  between primary and secondary texts, and to capture syntactic and
  morphological features that can be used in natural language
  processing.  Again, the heterogeneity of encoding practices applied
  to particular texts for particular purposes has made it challenging
  to work with the corpus as a whole.

- Normalization of TEI Usage :: The first TEI schema was developed in
  1987, and it has undergone many changes since them, some large and
  some small.  As the schema has evolved, so too have "best practices"
  for using it: recommended uses for elements and attributes to
  capture particular features.  In many cases, new elements and
  attributes have been added to the schema to facilitate uses that
  were awkward or impossible before.  Perseus's text base embodies the
  history of this evolution, and one of the more delicate parts of the
  normalization project is identifying long-used encoding patterns for
  which there are now better, or more accepted alternatives.

  The development of Perseus-specific schemas is part of this
  normalization process.  While we have crafted the Perseus schemas to
  be rather loose to start with -- in order to facilitate the release
  of Minimum Viable Perseus -- our aim is to refine the schemas to
  capture all and only the elements, attributes, and attribute values
  germane to the Perseus corpora.  This has long been the chief
  function of schemas: to assist editors in the consistent encoding of
  texts with schema-aware software.

  The remainder of this document is a survey of the corpus, conducted
  using Claude.  It should be read as one would read any AI-generated
  report: sometimes overly detailed, sometimes pedantic; sometimes
  misleading.  It is, however, a useful snapshot of where the
  normalization project stands today.

** Citation Structure Subclasses
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: citation-structure-subclasses
:END:
The normalization pipeline assigns each file a =proposed_subclass= --- a
finer classification within its genre family --- which determines the
citeStructure template injected by =add-citeStructure.xsl=. The
subclasses observed across the Greek canonical corpus are:

| Subclass                | Count | Description                                     |
|-------------------------+-------+-------------------------------------------------|
| =prose-section=         | 863   | Prose with section-level citation only          |
| =prose-chapter-section= | 198   | Prose with chapter → section hierarchy          |
| =prose-standard=        | 143   | Prose with book → chapter → section             |
| =verse-stichic=         | 137   | Verse with line-level citation                  |
| =drama-line=            | 84    | Drama with act → scene → line                   |
| =prose-chapter-verse=   | 69    | Prose with chapter → verse (primarily biblical) |
| =prose-book-section=    | 46    | Prose with book → section                       |
| =prose-book-chapter=    | 28    | Prose with book → chapter                       |
| =prose-chapter=         | 21    | Prose with chapter-level citation only          |
| =verse-book-line=       | 12    | Verse with book → line hierarchy                |

The Latin canonical corpus follows the same subclass taxonomy.

** Deferred Files and Known Problem Categories
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: deferred-files-and-known-problem-categories
:END:
Files were deferred (not normalized) for the following structural
reasons. These are not random failures --- they are coherent categories
that require decisions before normalization can proceed.

*** =jebb_page= (54 files, canonical-greekLit)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: jebb_page-54-files-canonical-greeklit
:END:
The Sophocles texts (tlg0284.*) use a page-based citation scheme derived
from Jebb's edition: =milestone[@unit='jebb_page']= rather than
act/scene/line. This is a legitimate but non-standard scheme that the
current citeStructure templates don't handle.

*** =fragment= (67+ files, multiple repos)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: fragment-67-files-multiple-repos
:END:
Fragment collections don't fit the standard hierarchical citeStructure
patterns. They may be single =<div type="textpart" subtype="fragment">=
with no subdivisions, or complex multi-level fragment collections.
Decisions needed per collection; no one-size-fits-all template exists.

*** =chapter+verse= (14 files, canonical-greekLit)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: chapterverse-14-files-canonical-greeklit
:END:
Primarily biblical texts (Hebrew Bible translations). The chapter/verse
structure is standard but the current pipeline doesn't generate a
=prose-chapter-verse= citeStructure template with sufficient confidence
to normalize automatically. Review each work and confirm the assignment.

*** =bekker_page+book+l= (2 files)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: bekker_pagebookl-2-files
:END:
Bekker page milestones --- the standard citation scheme for Aristotle.
Like Jebb pages, these are real scholarly citation units that appear as
milestones in the text.  We need to develop a standard way of encoding
edition-page milestones (see, for example, [[https://github.com/PerseusDLCode/MinimumViablePerseus/discussions/42][another discussion]]).

*** =(none)= / =work= / =sentence= / =paragraph= / =section= (Latin: 42+)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: none-work-sentence-paragraph-section-latin-42
:END:
These are texts with either no structural subdivision (flat editions,
single =<body>= with no =<div>=) or atypical single-level structures.
The CSEL corpus in particular has 20 flat =<p[@n]>= editions
(=csel-flat-review.csv=) where the citation scheme is
=edition → paragraph= with no further hierarchy. These require
individual editorial decisions about the appropriate citeStructure.

*** =epistle= / =letter= (11+ files)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: epistle-letter-11-files
:END:
Epistolary texts that may use letter number as the primary citation
unit, sometimes combined with section or verse. Need a
=prose-epistolary= subclass and template.

*** =book+card= (4 files, canonical-greekLit)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: bookcard-4-files-canonical-greeklit
:END:
The card milestone scheme from the Homer texts (Iliad, Odyssey) --- a
Perseus-specific citation unit that divides books into “cards” for
paging. These predate citeStructure.


** Open Questions and Next Steps

*** Immediate (before any further normalization)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: immediate-before-any-further-normalization
:END:

- Decide on =jebb_page= and =bekker_page= :: Add citeStructure templates
  for these scholarly citation units, then normalize the 54 Sophocles
  and 2 Aristotle files.

- Process =chapter+verse= files :: These are likely straightforward;
  confirm the genre assignment and normalize.

*** Schema work (medium term)
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: schema-work-medium-term
:END:

- Tailor the schemas to fit desired encoding practices.
- Develop Schematron rules to facilitate encoding correction.

*** Reference Works
Reference works like LSJ, Allen & Greenough, and commentaries are
structurally far more complex than the primary texts and have decades
of divergent encoding history. They are out of scope for the current
normalization effort and require dedicated design work before any
pipeline can touch them.
